1The world this problem lives in

Buddhism began in northern India around the fifth century BCE. Its teachings were preserved in Indian languages — Sanskrit, Pāli, and several regional languages of the Indian subcontinent and Central Asia. For the first few centuries they were memorised and recited; written texts came later.

Buddhism reached China in the first and second centuries CE, along the trade routes through Central Asia. It arrived as texts in foreign languages, and so the history of Chinese Buddhism begins as a history of translation. From roughly 150 CE to 1000 CE, thousands of Indian Buddhist texts were rendered into Chinese. Many of the Indian originals were later lost; for a large part of early Buddhist literature, the Chinese translation is now the only surviving version. That makes these translations primary sources for the history of Buddhism itself, not just for China.

The translated texts were gathered, over centuries, into a canon: a layered collection that holds translations alongside commentaries, catalogues, Chinese compositions, and works whose status was contested at the time and since. It was copied by hand, then carved on woodblocks and printed, in several editions in China, Korea and Japan. The edition scholars standardly cite is the Taishō edition (大正新脩大藏經), compiled in Tokyo between 1924 and 1934. It numbers every text: T1, T2, … up to about T2920. When you see T0603, that is "Taishō text number 603." The Taishō was digitised by CBETA (Chinese Buddhist Electronic Text Association, Taiwan) from the late 1990s; CBETA's files are the machine-readable text most projects work from, including us. A sūtra is a scripture presented as the words of the Buddha or his disciples; most of the texts here are sūtras.

Why anyone cares who translated a text

Three reasons, and you should be able to say all three.

  1. Dating. Translators have known activity dates. If a text really was translated by An Shigao, a Chinese version existed by about 170 CE; if by Kumārajīva, by about 410. Since the Indian originals are often lost and undated, the Chinese translation's date is frequently the firmest date a Buddhist text has, and it holds only as long as the attribution does. Arguments about when a doctrine emerged rest on such dates.
  2. Doctrinal history. Translators and their teams shaped what they translated — which terms they chose, what they cut, what they added. Knowing which translation enterprise produced a text is a clue to the milieu and the vocabulary of ideas it came through, though genre, source language and later revision all intervene.
  3. The history of the Chinese language. These translations are the largest body of dated, vernacular-tinged Chinese prose from their period. Linguists use them to trace how the language changed. Wrongly dated texts corrupt that record.

The catch: many traditional attributions do not hold up. Medieval Chinese catalogues assigned translators to texts, sometimes centuries after the fact and on evidence of very uneven quality, and modern scholarship has been revising those assignments for a century. This is the field called philology — the study of texts as historical objects: who wrote them, when, how they were copied, what was changed along the way.

Michael Radich and what he asked

Michael Radich (Heidelberg) is one of the leading scholars doing this work for early Chinese Buddhist translations. His method is largely lexical: a translator has habits — favourite words, favourite ways of rendering a technical term, favourite sentence patterns — and those habits leave fingerprints. He and a collaborator built a software tool called TACL that compares texts by the strings of characters they share, and he has spent years building lists of those fingerprints. He wrote to the NTU group with three problems. We are presenting the first.

Editions, and why eight files are two witnesses

The canon was printed several times: the Song, Yuan and Ming dynasty editions in China, the Goryeo edition in Korea, a palace edition, and others. Each text's directory holds one file per edition. For one short text (T0237, a version of the Diamond Sūtra, about 6,400 Chinese characters) we compared all eight editions character by character. They fall into two families: in one the editions differ by at most 3 characters, in the other by up to 25; between the families by at least 56. Two families means two branches of the copying tree — two independent lines of transmission, not eight. Philologists have known this for decades; we did not discover it. We use it as the clearest small example of a principle that runs through Cohort: agreement between copies of the same source is not independent confirmation.

4The question, and why it is hard

Radich's question 1, verbatim

"Can we work out methods or tools that will help us to determine accurate ascriptions for texts in 'grey'?"

Note the verb: help us to determine. He is not asking for a machine that names translators. He is asking for tools. That distinction is the spine of the talk.

Why it is hard, in five facts you should be able to recite:

  1. No ground truth on the target. The 968 grey texts have no correct answer to check against. Any accuracy we quote is on the labelled texts, and it does not transfer.
  2. The classes are wildly imbalanced. After dropping texts under 2,000 characters (too short to profile) and translators with fewer than five units (too few to learn from), 368 units in 13 classes remain. Saṅghadeva has 108 units; four classes have exactly 5. Almost everything we drop (964 of 1,367 labelled units) is short.
  3. Some grey texts have no translator to find. A few are compositions in Chinese, not translations at all. A method that always names a translator will name one for those too.
  4. The labels are a scholar's judgment. Excellent judgment, but a classifier trained on them inherits every position in them, including contested ones.
  5. Texts quote each other. Commentaries quote the texts they comment on; later sūtras quote earlier ones. Shared words may mean shared hand — or quotation. The demo turns on exactly this.

5What "counting strings" means

Classical Chinese has no spaces and no agreed word boundaries, so the natural unit of text is the character, and the natural feature is a short run of characters — two, three or four in a row. We call these strings (in NLP terms, character n-grams). Radich's own tool works the same way.

The method has three steps.

  1. Build a profile for each translator. Take every securely labelled unit by An Shigao, count how often each string in the vocabulary occurs, and sum. That table of counts is An Shigao's profile. Do the same for the other twelve.
  2. Count the same strings in the text you are asking about.
  3. Ask which profile the text's counts fit best. We use two textbook classifiers so that the result is about the data and not about a classifier: naive Bayes (which translator's string distribution makes these counts most probable?) and nearest centroid (which translator's average profile points in the same direction, by cosine?). They agree on Radich's vocabulary (50% and 52% with works held out) and diverge on the label-free one (38% for nearest centroid, 48% for naive Bayes), which is why both are reported.

The output is not a verdict. It is a ranking of the 13 translators with a margin between first and second, plus the list of strings that pulled the text each way, with how often each occurs in each profile. Everything is a count someone can check by searching the text. That is the property Radich's community trusts, and it is why we did not replace it with a black box.

6The mistake that read as 82%

To measure a classifier you hold some data out, train on the rest, and test on what you held out. The first thing any of us does is hold out one item at a time. We did, and got 82%. Then we held out one work at a time — all the chapters of a book together — and got 52%.

The gap is called leakage: information about the test item was sitting in the training data. When you hold out chapter 40 of T26 and train on chapters 1–39 and 41–108, the classifier learns to recognise T26 — its subject, its source text, its translation session's vocabulary — and chapter 40 is trivially "the same book." It was never recognising Saṅghadeva. Proof: under the honest split Saṅghadeva falls from 95% to 3%, because when T26 is withheld only two units of him remain to learn from. Every translator whose units are one-per-work is unchanged between the two splits; every one with many units per work drops.

The first number we got was 82 percent. It was wrong — not because of a bug, but because Radich's corpus splits long books into chapters, and a classifier that has seen 107 chapters of a book recognises the 108th. When we hold out whole books, the number is 52. Everything else tonight is built on the honest split.

This is expected practice in authorship attribution, so present it not as a discovery about method but as a fact about this corpus: its unitisation makes the naive number look publishable, and any group working with Radich's files will hit the same trap.

8Embeddings: what they are, what they found

An embedding model (also "encoder") is a neural network that turns a passage of text into a list of numbers — a vector — arranged so that passages with similar meaning get similar vectors. You can then ask "which passages in the corpus are most similar to this one" by comparing vectors. This is the deep-learning component of the demo.

We used the two current encoders built specifically for Buddhist texts, from the Dharmamitra project: a 2-billion-parameter and a 9-billion-parameter model (a parameter is one learned number; billions of them is what "large" means), trained on Buddhist Sanskrit, Tibetan, Chinese and Pāli. General-purpose multilingual encoders were not candidates: by the Dharmamitra model card's own benchmark, on Buddhist parallel passages a strong general encoder finds the right passage first 0.7% of the time where these find it 77–85% of the time (we have not re-run that benchmark; attribute it to them). We trained nothing. We cut every text in the corpus into 400-character windows (54,616 windows) and ran them through each frozen model on the lab's GPU: 36 minutes for the small one, two hours for the large.

Finding 2: they find what the strings cannot

We asked the 9B model for the nearest passages to each of the 30 windows of T0603, An Shigao's Yin chi ru jing 陰持入經 — a short treatise on the Buddhist analysis of experience into aggregates, elements and sense-fields. Twenty-five of the thirty pointed to the same place: T1694, a text Radich labels pre-Dhr-other. Query and neighbour visibly shared phrases, e.g. 譬是人為多熱，如是名遍.

T1694 is 陰持入經註 — the commentary on T0603, from the third century, which quotes the sūtra phrase by phrase and explains it. Radich knows this; his catalogue even has a bin named pre-T1694. We measured it: with CBETA's editorial punctuation and line breaks removed, 65% of all ten-character strings in T0603 occur verbatim in T1694, with a longest shared run of 279 characters. Against every other unit in the corpus (2,305 of them) the next highest overlap is 0.21%.

Now recall that under Radich's vocabulary the string method got T0603 wrong: it said pre-Dhr-other rather than An Shigao. We can now say exactly why. The three strongest strings for pre-Dhr-other were 道迹 (10 occurrences in that profile), 現色 (6) and 卒不 (3) — and in this corpus build every one of those occurrences is in T1694. T1694 supplies 223 of the profile's 1,012 feature tokens. Withhold T1694 and recompute: the leaning toward pre-Dhr-other (margin +1.70) collapses to +0.10 for An Shigao — which by the tab's own rule is "nothing either way", not a confident An Shigao. That is the honest result: Radich's 45 hits in this text never had anything to say about An Shigao; they were quotation. Under the label-free vocabulary An Shigao already led before the test, and afterwards the runner-up simply changes from pre-Dhr-other to Zhi Qian. This is a sensitivity analysis, not a proof of who translated T0603.

The encoders cannot tell translators apart — we measured that carefully, and it is a result. But they are very good at finding passages that say the same thing. That is how they surfaced the commentary quoting the text we were trying to attribute — a relation a specialist knows, recovered from the text alone and without being told — which is exactly the kind of dependency that fools a counting method. The two instruments fail at each other's job, and that is why the demo uses both.

9A trained classifier, honestly

Someone will ask why we count instead of training. We did train, in the two ways that are honest with 368 units:

  • A linear probe on the 9B vectors — a single trained layer on top of the frozen encoder, whole works held out. 39.4%. Translator identity is faintly present in the vectors, just not in a way similarity search can see.
  • A linear model on character 1–3-grams, learning its own feature weights from the 400-character windows, works held out in five grouped folds (so not the same protocol as the 52%), a unit's answer being the majority vote of its windows, feature weighting fitted inside each fold. 56.8% — the best number so far, in under a minute on a CPU, and still fully inspectable: the learned weights are strings.

Two things to say about it. First, trained beats counted by about four points under a slightly different fold scheme — real, not transformative; both are bounded by the same corpus, and Saṅghadeva is 0–4% under every method because one work is nearly all there is of him. Second, we did not fine-tune a deep model, and both outside reviewers were emphatic that we should not: thirteen classes, five of them with five examples, would overfit and trade the one property this audience trusts — every feature is a string you can search — for a vector nobody can inspect.

10The colour-coded text, how to read it

Yes — this is in the demo. It is the Evidence tab in Cohort. For any text in the corpus, grey or labelled, it paints the evidence directly onto the characters.

何等為四意止。從是身觀身止。道迹者。名為四意止。

Illustrative fragment and strip. In the live tab every colour is computed from counts.

What you are looking at, element by element:

The two colours
The painting compares a fixed pair, A (teal) against B (rust). A is the text's own catalogue label when it has one — so a labelled text is always painted "for its translator against the strongest rival" — otherwise the leading candidate; B is the strongest other class. The pair stays fixed when you switch vocabularies, so a change of colour is a change of evidence: T0603 paints rust under Radich's strings and teal under the label-free ones. (An earlier version coloured whoever was winning teal, which made every text teal; that is fixed.)
Saturation
How much weight of evidence sits on that character. The weight of a string is its log-odds: the logarithm of how many times more likely it is in A's profile than in B's — a weight of +3 means about 20 times, +7 about 1,000 times. A string at 33 per 100,000 in one profile and 0 in the other carries a large weight; one at 12 and 11 carries almost none. Strings overlap (a 4-character string contains two 3-character strings), so weights are summed per character and overstate certainty; read them as a ranking.
The strip
The whole text, one cell per few hundred characters, coloured by the average evidence in that window; click a cell to read that part. A text that changes colour part-way changes something — most often its subject, sometimes its hand — and is worth opening at that point. It does not by itself prove a composite text.
The header line
Which translator leads, over whom, by what margin (a per-string log-odds figure that is not comparable across texts); how many strings were counted; which vocabulary was used; and — critically — how many units of the text's own work were withheld from the profiles before counting, with a table of how much of each class is left. That is the leakage rule from section 6, enforced on every query. A box below it lets you withhold further units by id and recompute: the sensitivity test.
The two tables
The strongest strings pulling each way: the string, how many times it occurs in this text, its raw count and its rate per 100,000 feature tokens in each of the two profiles, and its weight. The raw count matters: "988 per 100,000" in pre-Dhr-other is ten occurrences in a profile of 1,012 tokens. This is the part a philologist actually reads.
The vocabulary switch
Radich's strings / label-free strings / both. Flip it and watch the picture change. On T0603: his strings, 45 hits, leans pre-Dhr-other (wrong; the page is rust); label-free, about 2,800 hits, leans An Shigao (right; the page is teal).
The ledger
A table of everything the profiling discarded and why — units under the length floor, translators with too few units, directories with no text. A reader chooses a text knowing how much never made it in.
"No evidence" and "not judged"
If not one string of the vocabulary occurs in the text, the tab says "no evidence" and ranks nobody — it does not pick a name out of a tie of zeros. A translator with no hits at all in the vocabulary is named as unjudged rather than silently ranked. Neither state arises for T0603; both exist because the method must refuse to answer when it has nothing.

What it is not. A margin near zero means "no evidence either way," not "a weak attribution." A text composed in Chinese has no translator to find, and if any vocabulary string occurs in it the tab will still show a leaning. The researcher reads; the panel only points. Say that sentence on stage.

11What Cohort is, in plain words

Cohort is the NTU group's research infrastructure: a system in which AI agents and a human researcher build an evidence graph together, under rules that never let the machine's output count as true until a person accepts it.

The graph

Nodes are things like: a witness (a source text, e.g. "T0603 in the Taishō edition"), a passage (a located span of a witness — this text, these characters), a claim (a statement about the texts), a conjecture (a claim proposed with a stated way to refute it), a question (what an inquiry was asking). Edges connect them: a passage attests a claim; a passage is part of a witness; a conjecture is tested by a query; one witness is a parallel of or descends from another.

The rules that matter for this talk

  • Nothing is asserted true. Every node has a status: proposed (an agent said it), attested (a different agent checked that its citations resolve to real text), accepted (the human researcher agreed). Only accepted nodes may be cited. The ladder only goes up through the human.
  • A claim must cite a located passage, and the system re-fetches the passage from the archive and checks the characters are there. A conjecture must state what would refute it. Writes that fail these rules are refused, and the refusals are logged as an output of the system — a zero refusals count is itself a fact.
  • An agent cannot check its own work, and the checking agent must be from a different model family.
  • Independence is computed, not assumed. If a claim is supported by two passages whose witnesses are connected by a parallel of or descends from edge, the system reports the support as not independent. Eight editions of the Diamond Sūtra count as two witnesses. A commentary that quotes its base text does not independently confirm anything about the base text. This is the rule the demo turns on.

Attestation versus witness

These two words come up constantly and they are not the same. An attestation is one passage cited in support of a claim. A witness is the source that passage comes from. You can have eight attestations from two witnesses. Cohort counts attestations, counts distinct witnesses, and then checks whether the witnesses are independent of one another — and reports all three separately, because a densely cited claim looks well supported whether or not its support is independent.

Agents and tools

Cohort's agents are language models that can call tools — functions the system exposes, such as "find passages attesting this phrase," "verify this exact span exists," "propose a conjecture," "link two witnesses as parallels." Every call is logged with its cost, and there is a hard budget per run. The researcher never sees the implementation; they see what the agent found, what it proposed, and what the system refused. The new tools built for this demo — evidence, semantic neighbours, passage alignment — plug into the same list.

11bThe five tabs, and what every colour means

Cohort's browser UI has five tabs. Each is switched on by a server flag, and a tab that is switched off is not greyed out but absent (the route is not even mounted). What follows is what each one shows, what you can do in it, and what its colours encode, so that nothing on the projector is a surprise.

Graph

Always on. The evidence graph itself. Since upstream's change of 6 September it is drawn on a canvas as a draggable, force-directed graph (the earlier version was a fixed left-to-right column layout): witnesses (source texts), passages (located spans of them), claims and conjectures, and research questions, pulled into place by a physics simulation that settles in a second or two. The graph fits itself to the window on load and resize; drag a node to untangle a cluster; click one to open the same inspector as before; a selection in Findings focuses the node on the canvas. Queries, verifications and decisions ("audit" nodes) are hidden by default behind a toggle in Settings: they are bookkeeping, not evidence. One consequence to know: the layout is no longer identical from run to run, so cite nodes by their ids, not by where they sit in a screenshot.

Node colour now says what a node is and, for claims, what the edges say about it: a witness is black, a passage or query blue, a question indigo, an audit node grey. A claim or conjecture is green only when at least two independent witnesses corroborate it; yellow when nothing attests it, when it is single-sourced, or when its witnesses collapse to one through a discounting edge; red when it is contradicted or rejected. That verdict is computed on the client from the edges, so accepting a parallel_of between two witnesses can turn a green claim yellow in front of the room — which is the independence rule made visible.

Edge colours are a vocabulary, and the legend lists only what is actually drawn:

  • ■ blue edge — attests: this passage supports that claim. Adds support.
  • ■ orange, dashed, heavier — parallel of / descends from: two witnesses are not independent (copies, or the same passage in two texts). Discounts support: a claim backed by eight editions of one printing has one witness, not eight. The legend says the word "discounts" in bold so nobody reads the thickness as strength.
  • ■ red — contradicts: two assertions cannot both hold. Drawn as prominently as agreement.
  • ■ violet, dotted — tests: the query that would refute a conjecture, recorded before the evidence was in. Dotted because it is the one relation in the picture that is still open.
  • ■ cyan — addresses: which research question an assertion answers.
  • ■ grey — structural (part of, verifies, searched for, supersedes, quotes): where a record sits and how it was reached.

Node status rides the border, separately from the fill: proposed is dashed (unchecked), attested is solid, accepted is heavier (the only citable state), rejected is red-bordered and keeps a struck-through title in the inspector. Clicking any node opens its provenance: who wrote it, what attests it, how it was verified, and the independence check — attestations counted, distinct witnesses counted, and whether those witnesses are independent of one another. The "refused writes" counter at the top is always visible while the graph is; a zero is a fact worth showing.

Findings

Always on. Every claim and conjecture as a hypothesis, and beside them what the researcher has accepted (the only citable nodes) and what they rejected, with reasons. Rejections sit next to findings on purpose: conclusions without the discards would misrepresent the record. The list is not ranked — ordering by how much attests a hypothesis would be a confidence score under another name. Opening a hypothesis shows its dossier: how it was derived, the corpus boundary it was framed against, the selection risks, the alternative explanations that would account for the same evidence, the prior-art search actually run, the prediction recorded at proposal time and what happened when its query was run, the evidence with excerpts, and the verifications — with the machine's finding and the reviewer's reading in separate fields. Two integrity checks live here too, on demand: re-hash every stored payload, and replay the event log to confirm the database matches it.

Corpus

With --corpus. Browse and search the source texts. Search is exact substring over every citable span — no wildcards, no stemming — and results come back in corpus order, not ranked: a relevance model would favour short commentaries over the scriptures they quote, a scholarly judgement smuggled into infrastructure, so the panel says "corpus order" in words and says how many hits were not shown. Opening a hit fetches the record with its length stated, and a toggle strips the TEI markup for reading — with a warning that stripped text no longer shares character offsets with the witness, so it is for reading, never for locating a span. Each hit links out to CBETA Online, and "send to agent" hands the phrase to the Inquiry tab as a task. Tonight this tab is off on the demo machine (section 15, and the CBETA note below).

Evidence

With --radich PATH. The colour-coded text from section 10 — where one text leans between translator profiles and which strings and spans make it lean, with the text's own work withheld, both vocabularies, a ledger of what was discarded, a sensitivity test (withhold further units and recompute), and provenance (which file, which hash) on every answer. Its colours are deliberately not the graph's: ■ teal pulls toward translator A, ■ rust toward translator B, the pair fixed as the text's catalogue label against its strongest rival. A leaning toward a candidate is not an edge and must not look like one.

Inquiry

With --allow-runs, which also needs a corpus and an OpenRouter key. The only tab that spends money. It opens on a research question — what is being asked, and what would count as an answer, stated before looking — and lets the researcher assemble a roster of agents and run them against it with a hard budget per run that the browser cannot raise. Two rules the roster enforces: an agent cannot check its own work, and the reviewing agent must come from a different model family than the proposing one. While a run proceeds the tab shows spend, tool calls, and refusals; what the agents propose lands in the graph at proposed, and the researcher accepts or rejects it in Findings. Nothing an agent writes becomes citable here.

Two words that are not the same

Attestation: one passage cited in support of a claim. Witness: the source that passage comes from. Eight editions of the Diamond Sūtra that all contain a line are eight attestations from two witnesses (two printing families). Cohort reports both counts, then whether the witnesses are independent — and only the last one is evidence.

Quotes versus descends from: descends from is a claim about copying — this manuscript or printing was made from that one, so their agreement is one witness twice. Quotes is a claim about use — this text reproduces words from that one, as a commentary does, so their shared wording is not two hands writing alike. Both make agreement non-independent, for different reasons. Cohort has both edges; at the moment only descends from and parallel of discount the independence count. T1694 quotes T0603; it does not descend from it. Whether quotes should also discount is a design decision to make before the talk, and the honest thing to say on stage is that the edge is recorded either way.

Under the hood: the agent called the tool attribution_evidence("T0603", "radich") and received exactly this table as data.
  2. The agent asks the encoder what else in the corpus reads like T0603. It calls semantic_neighbors("T0603"). For each 400-character window of T0603 the tool returns the three most similar windows anywhere else in the corpus, excluding T0603's own book. The answer, with CBETA's punctuation removed: 65% of T0603's ten-character strings occur verbatim in T1694; the longest shared run is 279 characters; the next-highest overlap with any other unit in the corpus is 0.21%. Whatever T1694 is, it contains most of T0603.
  4. The agent interprets — and is not allowed to name a translator. Under its contract it must propose at least two explanations, cite every observation to a located passage, and never conclude an attribution. It writes, in substance: "Explanation A: T0603 shares vocabulary with the pre-Dhr-other group because it was translated by someone in that group. Explanation B: T0603 shares vocabulary with pre-Dhr-other because that group contains T1694, which quotes T0603 at length — the shared strings are quotation, not a shared hand. B is supported by the 279-character verbatim run at these offsets. B predicts that withholding T1694 from the profile will remove the leaning toward pre-Dhr-other; A predicts it will not." It enters both into Cohort as conjectures, each with that refutation test attached. The relation itself, T1694 quotes T0603, is stated in the conjecture's prose; no tool can yet write it as an edge. (Cohort has a quotes edge, and as of PR #6 it discounts independence the way parallel_of does; only link_parallels writes discounting edges today, and only from CBETA's own cross-references. Do not describe the commentary with descends_from, which means a copy descending from an exemplar. For T0453 and chapter 48.3, which are one text, parallel_of is the edge.) All of it sits at proposed. This is the frontier model's role: reading the evidence a tool produced and writing the rival interpretations, grounded in cited text. It does not touch the counting.
  5. The test runs. The system re-executes the evidence with T1694 withheld — in the tab, type T1694 in the "also withhold" box and press recompute — or, on stage, open a pre-made link (#uid=T0603&features=radich&withhold=T1694) rather than typing live; in the terminal, cohort evidence T0603 --withhold T1694. Under the label-free vocabulary An Shigao already led; withholding T1694 only changes the runner-up to Zhi Qian. Explanation B's prediction held; A's did not. This is a sensitivity analysis: it shows the leaning depended on T1694, not that An Shigao translated T0603.

13How the system meets the abstract, today

The accepted abstract makes six promises. For each: what runs now, what the live run of 6 September showed, and the gap that remains. Nothing here describes a plan; "runs" means it ran.

"Agent-based architectures powered by large language models"

A run assembles a roster — a worker (meta/muse-spark-1.3) and a reviewer (x-ai/grok-4.6) — each a tool-use loop over a fixed list of tools, under a dollar budget per run that the browser cannot raise, with every model call logged with its cost. The reviewer must come from a different provider family and cannot attest what it authored. The live run: 12 tool calls in 73 seconds for $0.11, and the whole chain of calls was the model's choice, not a script.

"An interactive research environment"

Five tabs the researcher drives (section 11b): ask a question with its answer-condition stated before looking; run a roster against it; browse and search the corpus; paint any text's evidence, switch vocabularies, withhold a suspected dependency and recompute; read every hypothesis with its dossier; accept or reject. The Evidence tab's state lives in the URL, so a view is a link you can send.

"Agents assist with corpus retrieval"

Two kinds, both callable by the agent. Exact: find_attestations searches the corpus for a phrase and attaches the located passages to a claim — in the live run the worker found 色所更 in T0603, T1694 and T0222 and attached 53 witnesses. By meaning: semantic_neighbors, over a frozen Buddhist-Chinese encoder, returns the passages in other works that read like each window of a text — which is how the agent got from T0603 to T1694 without being told.

"Semantic analysis"

attribution_evidence gives where a text's short strings lean between translator profiles, with raw counts and a ledger of everything discarded; the encoder gives what a text is about, by likeness of content. The two disagree on purpose — strings see habit, vectors see subject — and the tools' own descriptions say so. Limit, stated: the encoder is at the largest-class baseline for translator identity (section 8) and is presented as content retrieval, never as attribution.

"Variant alignment"

align_passages measures verbatim overlap between two units on Han characters and returns the shared runs with offsets into each original text; the agent used it to establish 64.5% and a 279-character run between T0603 and T1694 and cited a shared string from it. The two tools that read CBETA's own apparatus and cross-references (collate_editions, link_parallels) need the CBETA XML archive rather than Radich's plain text; that archive (CBETA's xml-p5 at tag 2021Q3, the release matching v061) is in hand and its Taishō index is built, so they run when the server is pointed at it — at the price that the Corpus tab then shows CBETA's text while Evidence shows Radich's. Say which is on screen.

"Interpretation … provenance-aware … grounded in source texts"

This is what the architecture is built for. A claim cannot be written without a grounding query that hits the corpus — four of the worker's attempts were refused as UngroundedClaim, because a text's title occurs nowhere in its body. A conjecture cannot be written without alternative explanations and a stated refutation test — the worker's carried both (a shared hand, or quotation), tested by withholding T1694. Every cited passage is re-fetched and hash-verified: the reviewer withheld attestation on one claim because 5 of 53 spans did not re-locate, and returned indeterminate on the two new ones because the citations did not themselves carry the numbers claimed. Every Evidence answer names its file and SHA-256. The event log, not the database, is the record.

Two honest limits. "Grounded" means the cited words exist, not that they support the reading; the reviewer's judgment and the researcher's supply that. And grounding is existence, not relevance: the worker's third claim passed the boundary grounded on the single character 佛. That is a rule worth tightening, and a good thing to admit on stage before someone finds it.

"Integrating human expertise … a more dynamic and collaborative model"

Nothing an agent writes is ever more than proposed; only the researcher accepts, and rejections with their reasons are kept beside the findings. The researcher's own moves are first-class: the question with its answer-condition, the withholding test, the vocabulary switch. The system's characteristic output is its refusals — five in the live run, each named and categorised — a record of where the machine was stopped, not a list of errors. Gap: accepting an edge or conjecture straight from the Evidence tab is not built; you accept in Findings.

"Diachronic"

The weakest fit. The only time dimension is Radich's historical ordering of the translators, shown beside each candidate as "#11 of 21 in Radich's order". There is no dating model. If pressed, say "dated by ascription", not "diachronic analysis".

The agents did the retrieval, the alignment and the drafting. The rules stopped what they could not cite. A second model refused to promote what the citations did not carry. And the researcher decided — with the whole chain, its cost and its refusals, on the record.

13bThe story, and the wow moment

An academic talk earns a "wow" the way a good paper does: by setting up an expectation carefully enough that the audience feels the moment it breaks. The material has one of those, and the arc that delivers it runs in five movements; the minute-by-minute running order is in section 0.

  1. The trap (3 min). Radich's question, his corpus, and the number any of us would get first: 82%. Let it sit for a beat. Then the honest split: 52%, and Saṅghadeva falling from 95% to 3% because one book is all there is of him. The audience — DH people, librarians, East Asianists — will recognise the shape: the data looked better than it was, for a reason specific to how this corpus is cut. Nobody in the room is exempt from that trap; that is why it lands.
  2. The instrument (5 min). The vocabulary control. Radich's list wins overall and loses on the earliest translators; a label-free list does the reverse. Show the per-class table, then the Evidence tab on T0603 and flip the vocabulary switch live: rust to teal, wrong to right, on one page of text. The lesson is not "the list is bad." It is every instrument sees some hands better than others, and now that is measured rather than suspected.
  3. The known answer (3 min). Before the humbling, one text the specialists in the room already know: T0453, the Maitreya sūtra the Taishō gives to Dharmarakṣa and the field has denied him since 1935. The strings lean Zhu Fonian; the encoder sends every one of its ten windows to chapter 48.3 of his Ekottarika-āgama; alignment shows 93% of the text in shared runs; withhold the chapter, then the whole collection, and the lean survives. Say: the tool was not told any of this. The room now believes the instrument, and you have shown it once on a case with a known answer before you show it on a case where it was wrong.
  4. The humbling (2 min). The deep-learning result, told straight. A 9-billion-parameter model trained on exactly this literature, asked whether the passage most like this one is by the same translator: 23.7%, against 24.0% for always guessing the largest class. Seven thousand counted strings beat it by sixteen points on the unit-level task. Do not soften it — the room has spent two years being told large models solve everything, and an honest negative result from people who clearly wanted the model to work buys the credibility the next movement spends. End on the question it leaves hanging: so what does a model like that see, if not the translator?
  5. The wow (6 min). Answer it on screen. T0603 is up, rust under Radich's strings. Ask the encoder for the passages in the corpus most like T0603's — twenty-five of its thirty windows come back from T1694, with the same phrases visible in both. Alignment: a 279-character run shared verbatim, 65% of the text's ten-character strings; the next closest text in the whole corpus shares 0.2%. T1694 is the commentary on T0603 — it quotes the sūtra it explains, and Radich's catalogue already has a bin named after it. Every one of the ten occurrences of 道迹 that made the page rust is in T1694. The two readings and the test that separates them; the test runs, T1694 is withheld, and the page goes from rust to faintly teal in front of the room — not a confident An Shigao, but no case against him, which is the truthful outcome. Then the line that makes it land: the model that cannot tell translators apart is the one that surfaced the reason the counting was wrong. It saw content where the strings saw style, and that is exactly what a quotation is. The researcher accepts the conjecture; with the dependency recorded as an edge the graph would report these two texts as non-independent witnesses, with every step cited, and the machine has asserted nothing. (The edge tool is not built; say so rather than implying the run wrote it.)

Why this is a deserved wow and not a trick: nothing in it is invented. The 82% was really our first number; the encoder really is at the largest-class baseline; T1694 really surfaced from a retrieval we ran to test something else; the recomputation is one anyone can rerun from the tab. The audience watches a failure become an explanation, and the explanation is one a philologist would have given — the difference is that the system surfaced it from the text, said why, and waited for permission. The agent's turn is real too, as of 7 September; keep the recorded trace as the fallback for a bad network, and say which you are showing. That is the abstract's sentence about "reconfiguring the relationship between scholars and textual data," shown rather than claimed.

Close (2 min): what the graph now knows, what it refused to assert, and what a researcher gets — a tool that will catch the next hidden dependency in the next grey text, with the same discipline. Rehearse a ten-minute cut as well: the trap, the flip, T0453 and the humbling in one slide each, then the T0603 walk.

Two years ago you were told models would read these texts for you. Here is a nine-billion-parameter model that cannot tell An Shigao from Kumārajīva. And here is the same model catching a commentary quoting its sūtra — the one thing the counting method could never see — and handing the decision back to the scholar with the evidence attached. That is what we mean by a tool.

15Questions from the floor, with answers

"So does the system tell us who translated the grey texts?"

No, and it is built not to. It shows where the evidence points, what pulls it there, what else in the corpus might explain that, and it records the scholar's decision. Radich asked for methods that help determine ascriptions. This helps; it does not determine.

"Your accuracy is 52%. Why should I trust anything it says about grey?"

You shouldn't trust its leanings; you should read its evidence. 52% is a ceiling on the labelled texts, which are the easier problem. We quote it only to show how inflated the naive 82% was. Nothing in the demo reports a number on grey.

"Radich already does all this by hand with TACL."

Much of it, yes — the string comparison especially. What he does not have: the leakage control enforced on every query; evidence painted onto the text rather than listed beside it; a measured account of his own vocabulary's blind spots; a neural retriever that finds quoting and quoted texts; and a graph that remembers which witnesses are not independent, so the same mistake is not made twice.

"You have rediscovered genre and quotation and called it translator style."

Partly — and that is the point of the infrastructure. The counting finds where a contrast arises; the neighbours and the alignment find whether it is idiom, content, or quotation; the scholar decides. The T0603 case is precisely a contrast that turned out to be quotation, surfaced by the system without being told. Radich's catalogue has a class named pre-T1694, so the relation was known to him; what is new is that the machine recovered it from counts and acted on it.

"Why not fine-tune a language model on the translators?"

Thirteen classes, five of them with five examples, and no ground truth on the target. A fine-tuned model would overfit the same book-level structure we just caught, would be unevaluable on grey, and would trade a searchable string for an uninspectable vector. We did train a linear model on strings — 56.8%, four points better than counting — and stopped there on purpose.

"Isn't the LLM just going to write plausible nonsense about the numbers?"

If handed a frequency table and asked for a verdict, yes. So it is not asked for one. Its contract: at least two explanations, every observation cited to a located span, a refutation test for each, no attribution, no probability. Its output enters the graph as proposed and only a human can move it.

"Whose labels are these? Are they right?"

Michael Radich's, and we chose them because the field would. They are one scholar's considered positions, including contested ones — T125 as Zhu Fonian rather than Saṅghadeva, for instance. The method inherits the labels; it cannot outrun them, and we say so.

"Why are eight editions two witnesses?"

Because they fall into two families that descend from two lines of transmission. Within one family the editions differ by at most three characters in some 6,400; within the other by up to 25; between the families by at least 56. Agreement between copies of the same line is not independent confirmation. Cohort computes this rather than assuming it.

"T0453 is a textbook case — everyone knows it is not Dharmarakṣa. What have you shown?"

That the instrument reaches the textbook answer from the text alone, with its reasons showing, in three tool calls and under a minute — and that it reaches it the way a philologist would: the strings point at Zhu Fonian, the retriever finds the chapter, the alignment shows it is a copy, and the sensitivity test says how much of the lean is the copy and how much is the rest of his work. A known case is the only kind on which a new instrument can be checked. The next grey text will not be a textbook case, and the same four steps will run on it.

"Isn't 'leans Zhu Fonian' just 'reads like an āgama'?"

Partly, and we say so. The heaviest label-free strings for him are narrative formulae — 爾時, 是時, 來至我所. That is why the test withholds the whole Ekottarika and not just the chapter: the lean falls from +1.18 to +0.53 and now rests on his Dīrghāgama, which is the same genre. So the honest statement is "consistent with Zhu Fonian, and with the genre he mostly translated"; the thing the graph records is the copy relation, which does not depend on that distinction.

"Why is T0453 grey if the field agrees?"

Because the field agrees on a negative — not Dharmarakṣa — and on a parallel, not on a translator. Radich's grey means "not securely ascribed to a named translator in my tables". Whether the stand-alone sūtra is Zhu Fonian's hand, an extract someone made from his collection, or an older version he absorbed, is open; the tool's output is compatible with all three and the graph records only what was shown.

"What is T26 and why does it keep coming up?"

The Madhyama-āgama, a collection of 222 discourses translated by Saṅghadeva around 397 CE, split by Radich into about 108 units. It is almost all of Saṅghadeva we have. Hold out one chapter and the classifier recognises the book; hold out the book and there is no Saṅghadeva left to learn. It is the cleanest example of leakage in the corpus.

17Glossary

Āgama
One of the large collections of early Buddhist discourses (the Chinese counterparts of the Pāli Nikāyas). T26 is the Madhyama-āgama.
ascription / attribution
The assignment of a text to an author or translator. Radich says ascription; NLP says attribution. Same thing.
attestation
One passage cited in support of a claim. Distinct from witness.
canon
The authorised collection of Buddhist scriptures. The Chinese canon was printed in several editions; the Taishō is the modern standard.
CBETA
Chinese Buddhist Electronic Text Association; the digitised Taishō everyone uses.
character n-gram / string
A run of n consecutive characters. We use 2, 3 and 4. Classical Chinese has no word boundaries, so this is the natural feature.
commentary
A text that explains another text, usually quoting it phrase by phrase. T1694 is the commentary on T0603.
composite work
One Taishō number containing several originally separate texts, possibly by different translators. T310 has 49.
control
An experiment that changes one variable and holds all others fixed.
edition
One historical printing of the canon (Song, Yuan, Ming, Goryeo, …). Each unit's directory has one file per edition.
embedding / encoder
A neural model that maps a passage to a vector so that similar-meaning passages are near each other.
grey
Radich's label for units whose translator is not securely known. 968 of them. The target.
independent support
Cohort's check that the witnesses behind a claim are not copies of, or quotations from, one another.
largest-class baseline
What you score by always answering with the most frequent class. The honest "chance" for an imbalanced problem; here 24.0% at window level, 29.3% at unit level.
leakage
Test-set information present in training data — here, other chapters of the same book.
leave-one-work-out
Evaluation in which every unit of the test item's book is removed from training. The honest split.
log-odds
The logarithm of a ratio of probabilities; here, the log of how many times more likely a string is in one profile than another (+3 ≈ 20×, +7 ≈ 1,000×). Positive pulls one way, negative the other; summed over a text, it is the classifier's score.
Mahāyāna
"Great Vehicle": the strand of Buddhism that became dominant in East Asia. Lokakṣema was its first translator into Chinese.
naive Bayes / nearest centroid
Two simple classifiers over counts (section 5).
paratext
Prefaces, afterwords, colophons — material around a text rather than of it. Radich stripped it.
philology
The study of texts as historical objects: authorship, date, transmission, variation.
profile
A translator's table of string counts over their securely ascribed units.
proposed / attested / accepted
Cohort's status ladder: said by an agent / citations checked by a different agent / agreed by the human. Only accepted is citable.
recall@1
How often the correct item is the first result a retrieval system returns.
recension
A branch of a text's copying history. The eight editions of T0237 form two.
refutation condition
What a conjecture says would prove it wrong, stated before looking. Cohort refuses conjectures without one.
sūtra
A Buddhist scripture presented as the words of the Buddha or his disciples.
TACL
Radich's own string-comparison software.
Table 1
Radich's 7,270 curated strings for a Dharmarakṣa dictionary; our first vocabulary.
Taishō
The standard modern edition of the Chinese Buddhist canon (Tokyo, 1924–34). "T0603" is its numbering.
unit / work
A work is one Taishō number; a unit is Radich's piece of it — the whole if short, a chapter or component if long or composite.
unitisation
How a corpus is cut into pieces. Radich's cuts long works into chapters; that choice is what makes leave-one-unit-out leak.
window
A 400-character slice of a text; the unit the encoder embeds and the trained model learns from.
witness
A source that carries a text — a manuscript, an edition, a quoting commentary. Distinct from attestation.
